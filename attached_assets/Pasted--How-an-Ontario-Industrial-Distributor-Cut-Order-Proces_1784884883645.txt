## The client

A family-owned distributor supplying industrial consumables, safety products, tools and maintenance materials to contractors, facilities teams and small manufacturers across Ontario.

### Company profile

* **Industry:** Industrial wholesale and distribution
* **Location:** Greater Toronto Area
* **Employees:** 42
* **Annual revenue:** Approximately C$14.8 million
* **Active business customers:** 640
* **Products:** Approximately 4,600 SKUs
* **Monthly orders:** Approximately 1,850
* **Warehouse locations:** Two
* **Sales channels:** Email, telephone, sales representatives and counter orders
* **Existing systems:** ERP, accounting software, shared inboxes and spreadsheets

The company had grown steadily through responsive service and long-standing customer relationships.

Customers knew they could email a purchase order, call an account representative or send a photograph of the part they needed. The sales team would work out the product, check the customer’s price, confirm availability and get the order moving.

That flexibility helped build the business.

It also created an operation that depended heavily on people remembering:

* Which customer received which price
* Which products could be substituted
* Which warehouse had stock
* Which orders needed approval
* Which account was on hold
* Which delivery promise had been made
* Which spreadsheet contained the latest information

The company had an ERP.

But the ERP was not where most orders began.

---

# The problem

Approximately 72% of orders arrived through email, PDF purchase orders, telephone calls or messages sent directly to sales representatives.

Every one of those orders had to be interpreted and entered manually.

A typical order required an employee to:

1. Open the customer’s email or purchase order.
2. Identify the correct customer account.
3. Search for each product code.
4. Resolve incomplete or outdated SKUs.
5. Check the customer’s contract price.
6. Verify available inventory.
7. Check credit status.
8. Confirm delivery requirements.
9. Enter the order into the ERP.
10. Send confirmation to the customer.
11. Notify the warehouse of any exception.

When the purchase order contained a discontinued code, ambiguous description or incorrect price, the process stopped.

The coordinator emailed the customer.

The customer contacted the sales representative.

The representative checked an old quotation or pricing spreadsheet.

The coordinator updated the order.

By then, the same transaction might have passed through four people and three systems.

Each system performed a function.

Employees still had to connect the functions manually.

# The complete pain points

## 1. Orders entered through too many channels

Customers could order by:

* Email
* PDF purchase order
* Telephone
* Sales representative
* Counter request
* Repeated previous order
* Spreadsheet attachment

The company valued customer flexibility and did not want to force every buyer into a rigid e-commerce process.

But the absence of a common intake workflow meant every channel created different work.

An order sent to an account representative’s personal inbox might not reach operations until the representative returned from a customer visit.

A purchase order sent to the general inbox might be opened by two coordinators.

A telephone order might contain a product description but no SKU.

There was no controlled queue showing:

* What had arrived
* Who owned it
* Whether it had been entered
* What information was missing
* Whether the customer had received confirmation

---

## 2. Customer pricing lived partly outside the ERP

The company served customers with different:

* Contract prices
* Volume discounts
* Product-group discounts
* Freight arrangements
* Project-specific quotations
* Promotional terms
* Payment terms

Some rules were stored in the ERP.

Others were recorded in spreadsheets, previous quotations, account notes or the sales representative’s memory.

This caused two kinds of error.

### Underpricing

The order was accepted at an outdated or overly generous price.

The customer was unlikely to complain, so the error could remain invisible.

### Overpricing

The customer received a price that did not match an earlier agreement.

The result was:

* Delayed approval
* A correction request
* A credit note
* An unhappy customer
* Additional work for sales, operations and finance

The problem became more serious when supplier costs changed quickly.

Statistics Canada found that 43% of Canadian wholesale businesses were affected by Canada–US trade tensions in April 2025. Common effects included price increases and higher raw-material, shipping and labour costs.

For a distributor importing products or sourcing from US suppliers, slow pricing updates were therefore not just an administrative problem. They created margin exposure.

---

## 3. Inventory availability was not the same as order availability

An employee could see inventory in the ERP.

That did not always answer whether the order could be fulfilled.

Stock might be:

* Reserved for another customer
* At the second warehouse
* Waiting for inspection
* Part of an incomplete kit
* Allocated to an open project
* Available only in a substitute brand
* Due from a supplier but not yet received

Sales representatives sometimes promised delivery based on the quantity displayed on screen without seeing these operational conditions.

The warehouse then had to manage the exception.

---

## 4. Product codes created avoidable friction

Customers frequently reordered using:

* Their own internal part numbers
* Old distributor product codes
* Supplier codes
* Descriptions copied from previous invoices
* Informal names used by site teams

Experienced coordinators knew how to translate these into the correct products.

New employees did not.

The knowledge required to process an order was concentrated in a small number of long-serving staff.

---

## 5. Status questions consumed the customer-service team

Customers regularly contacted the company to ask:

* Was the purchase order received?
* Has the order been entered?
* Is everything in stock?
* When will it ship?
* Is one item holding up the order?
* Has the back-ordered product arrived?
* Can the shipment be split?
* What is the tracking number?

The information existed somewhere.

Answering the question still required an employee to find it, interpret it and respond manually.

---

## 6. Management reporting was always retrospective

At the end of each week, the operations manager assembled reports from:

* ERP exports
* Open-order reports
* Warehouse spreadsheets
* Customer-service records
* Sales-team updates
* Back-order reports

The leadership team could see sales already booked.

It could not easily see:

* Orders waiting to be entered
* Revenue sitting in incomplete purchase orders
* Orders blocked by pricing questions
* Orders delayed by credit approval
* Customers repeatedly experiencing exceptions
* Products generating the most corrections
* Accounts receiving inconsistent pricing
* Workload by order coordinator

The company could measure what had been processed.

It could not see everything waiting before processing.

---

The distributor’s order volume had grown faster than its operating process.

At 1,850 orders per month, the same habits created:

* A growing shared inbox
* Longer confirmation times
* More pricing checks

* Increased dependence on senior employees

# How Pressense diagnosed the operation

The diagnostic followed actual orders from receipt through invoicing.

Rather than interviewing management alone, the process examined how coordinators, sales representatives, warehouse employees and finance staff handled the same transaction.

## We mapped six connected workflows

### 1. Order intake

How orders arrived, how they were acknowledged and how responsibility was assigned.

### 2. Product identification

How customer descriptions and product numbers were matched to the correct SKU.

### 3. Pricing

How customer-specific terms, quotations, discounts and supplier-cost changes were applied.

### 4. Inventory and fulfilment

How stock, substitutes, split deliveries and warehouse availability were confirmed.

### 5. Exception management

How incomplete, incorrect or blocked orders were resolved.

### 6. Customer communication

How confirmations, back-order updates, shipment notifications and status requests were handled.

---

## The central finding

The company did not have an order-entry problem.

It had an exception-management problem.

Straightforward orders were relatively easy.

The cost came from orders containing one or more uncertainties:

* Unknown product code
* Outdated price
* Missing delivery instruction
* Insufficient stock
* Credit hold
* Customer-specific substitution rule
* Minimum-order restriction
* Split-shipment decision
* Freight approval
* Unapproved discount

Every exception created a new conversation.

The order disappeared into email until someone resolved it.

Pressense therefore did not propose a conventional online store.

A conventional store would have forced every customer to search a large catalogue and understand the distributor’s SKU structure.

Instead, the company needed an operational layer capable of receiving orders in familiar formats, identifying straightforward transactions and routing only genuine exceptions to employees.

The time was distributed across order coordinators, sales representatives, warehouse supervisors, finance employees and management. It did not appear as a single obvious cost centre.

# What Pressense built

## A customer-order and operations control system

The system created one workflow between incoming customer demand and the company’s existing ERP.

It did not replace the ERP.

The ERP remained responsible for:

* Financial records
* Confirmed sales orders
* Inventory transactions
* Invoicing
* Customer balances
* Accounting

The new system managed everything required to turn an incoming request into a clean, validated order.

---

## 1. One order queue across every channel

Orders from approved email addresses, uploaded purchase orders, sales representatives and customer forms entered one controlled queue.

Each order received:

* A unique reference
* Customer account
* Date and time received
* Intake channel
* Assigned coordinator
* Processing status
* Exception status
* Requested delivery date
* Next action

The queue showed orders that were:

* New
* Being validated
* Waiting for customer information
* Waiting for sales approval
* Waiting for credit approval
* Ready for ERP submission
* Confirmed
* Partially fulfilled
* Complete

Nothing depended on whether the correct employee happened to see an email.

---

## 2. Purchase-order capture

The system extracted structured information from emailed and uploaded purchase orders:

* Customer purchase-order number
* Product code
* Description
* Quantity
* Requested price
* Delivery address
* Requested delivery date
* Special instructions

The extracted information was checked against business rules.

Any field with low confidence was highlighted for employee review.

The system did not silently convert uncertain information into an order.

It reduced data entry while keeping a person responsible for exceptions.

---

## 3. Customer-product mapping

The company created a translation layer connecting:

* Customer product codes
* Previous product codes
* Supplier product codes
* Distributor SKUs
* Approved substitute products

Once an employee resolved an unfamiliar customer code, the relationship was saved.

The next order from that customer could be recognised automatically.

This reduced the company’s dependence on employees remembering thousands of informal product relationships.

---

## 4. Pricing-rules engine

The pricing layer brought the company’s commercial rules into one controlled structure.

Rules could be set by:

* Customer
* Customer group
* Product
* Product category
* Quantity
* Contract
* Project
* Sales region
* Effective date
* Expiry date

The system compared the price on the incoming purchase order against the valid price.

### When the price matched

The order continued without intervention.

### When the price differed

The order was classified according to the reason:

* Customer using an expired price
* New supplier cost not yet reflected
* Discount outside approved range
* Project quotation still valid
* Customer contract requiring review
* Possible internal pricing error

Only the appropriate person was asked to decide.

---

## 5. Inventory and substitution rules

The system checked:

* Available stock
* Stock reserved for other orders
* Stock at each warehouse
* Incoming purchase orders
* Approved substitutes
* Customer substitution restrictions
* Partial-shipment rules
* Expected replenishment date

For common shortages, the coordinator could immediately see the approved next action.

Instead of beginning another internal email chain, the system presented options such as:

* Ship available quantity now
* Transfer from second warehouse
* Offer approved substitute
* Hold for complete shipment
* Escalate to account representative

---

## 6. Exception routing

Each exception had:

* A category
* An owner
* A deadline
* A required decision
* An escalation path

Pricing questions went to the appropriate sales representative.

Credit issues went to finance.

Product questions went to the product specialist.

Inventory exceptions went to the warehouse or purchasing team.

The order coordinator could see the entire status without repeatedly chasing each department.

---

## 7. Customer ordering portal

Repeat customers received access to a focused B2B portal.

It was not a public catalogue.

Each customer saw:

* Products previously purchased
* Customer-specific prices
* Frequently reordered items
* Approved substitutes
* Saved delivery locations
* Open orders
* Back-order status
* Previous invoices
* Shipment tracking
* Quick reorder options

Customers who preferred email could continue using email.

The system improved the email workflow rather than using the portal as a condition of service.

---

## 8. Sales-representative workspace

Sales representatives received a mobile-friendly view showing:

* Customer pricing
* Product availability
* Open orders
* Exceptions requiring approval
* Current quotations
* Account status
* Recent purchases

Representatives could create a structured order while visiting a customer instead of sending informal instructions to the order desk.

---

## 9. Operations dashboard

Management could see:

* Orders received today
* Orders awaiting entry
* Orders blocked by exception
* Orders waiting for customer response
* Orders waiting for internal approval
* Average order-processing time
* Orders approaching cut-off
* Corrections by category
* Pricing exceptions by account
* Back orders by value
* Coordinator workload
* Customer-service enquiries
* Order volume by channel

The dashboard showed work before it became an invoice.

# The redesigned workflow

## Before

**Purchase order arrives by email**

↓

Coordinator identifies customer and products

↓

Pricing spreadsheet or previous quotation is checked

↓

ERP stock is reviewed

↓

Questions are emailed to sales or warehouse

↓

Order waits in the inbox

↓

Employee follows up

↓

Order is entered manually

↓

Confirmation is emailed

↓

Customer later calls for status

## After

**Purchase order enters the order queue**

↓

Customer and order fields are captured

↓

Product codes are matched

↓

Pricing and stock rules are checked

↓

Only exceptions are assigned to employees

↓

Approved order is sent to the ERP

↓

Customer receives confirmation

↓

Order and shipment status remain visible

The company’s employees stopped manually processing every line as though every order were unusual.

They focused on the lines that genuinely required judgement.

# The outcome

## Order-processing time fell by 78%

Before the change, a manually received order took an average of approximately 18 minutes of active employee time across intake, validation and entry.

After the new workflow, straightforward orders required approximately four minutes.

[
\frac{18 - 4}{18} \times 100 = 77.8%
]

The result was a **78% reduction in active processing time per straightforward order**.

Complex orders still required human involvement. The system made those exceptions visible earlier and sent them directly to the person capable of resolving them.

---

That produced an **87% reduction in orders waiting more than one business day for confirmation**.

Before the system, only 54% of incoming orders received confirmation on the same business day.

After implementation, 94% were confirmed the same day.

The remaining 6% typically involved:

* Customer clarification
* Credit approval

* Special procurement requirements

The company could also tell the customer why an order was waiting rather than leaving the purchase order unacknowledged.

## Status enquiries fell by 71%

Monthly “where is my order?” and “did you receive my purchase order?” enquiries fell from approximately 420 to 120.

[
\frac{420 - 120}{420} \times 100 = 71.4%

Customer-service staff reclaimed approximately 25 hours each month from status enquiries alone.

More importantly, customers received information without needing to ask.

[
\frac{2,240 - 1,850}{1,850} \times 100 = 21.1%

The company did not add the order coordinator it had previously expected to recruit.

The existing team absorbed the higher volume because routine entry, pricing checks and status communication no longer consumed the same amount of time.

# Why the company did not replace its ERP

The ERP was not failing.

It remained capable of recording clean orders, inventory movements, invoices and financial transactions.

The weakness existed before information reached the ERP and after customers needed an update.

Replacing the ERP would have created:

* A longer implementation
* Greater migration risk
* More staff training
* Higher cost
* Disruption to finance and warehouse operations
* No guarantee that incoming orders would become more structured

Pressense therefore built the missing layer around the existing system.

That allowed the company to improve the workflow without rebuilding the entire technology stack.
